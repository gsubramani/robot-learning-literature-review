## 1. What Is Imitation Learning?

Imitation learning (IL) is the family of methods that trains a policy by observing an expert, rather than by specifying a reward function or coding hand-crafted rules. The core idea is disarmingly simple: record what an expert does, then train a model to reproduce it.

**Formal definition.** Given a dataset of expert demonstrations

```math
\mathcal{D} = \{(s_t, a_t)\}_{t=1}^{N}
```

where $`s_t \in \mathcal{S}`$ is the state at time $`t`$ and $`a_t \in \mathcal{A}`$ is the expert's action, the goal of imitation learning is to learn a policy $`\pi_\theta : \mathcal{S} \rightarrow \mathcal{A}`$ (or a distribution over actions) that behaves like the expert.

### How IL Differs from Neighboring Fields

| Paradigm | Supervision signal | State distribution during training | Requires environment interaction? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Supervised learning (e.g., ImageNet classification) | Labels on a static dataset | Fixed; i.i.d. from dataset | No |
| Reinforcement learning | Scalar reward $`r_t`$ from environment | Generated by the current policy | Yes |
| Behavior cloning (IL) | Expert actions $`a_t^*`$ | Fixed; i.i.d. from expert dataset | No |
| DAgger / interactive IL | Expert actions, queried online | Generated by the current policy | Yes (expert in the loop) |
| Inverse RL | Inferred reward from demonstrations | Mixed | Sometimes |

**Key distinction from supervised learning:** In ImageNet classification, the training and test distributions are the same by assumption. In IL, the policy is deployed *sequentially*—each action changes the state, so the distribution of states the policy encounters during deployment $`d_\pi`$ differs from the expert's distribution $`d_{\pi^*}`$. This train/test mismatch is the root cause of compounding errors (Section 7).

**Key distinction from RL:** RL requires a reward signal $`r(s, a)`$. For many real manipulation tasks—threading a needle, folding a shirt, surgical suturing—designing a reward function is as hard as solving the task itself. IL sidesteps reward engineering entirely, at the cost of requiring expert demonstrations.

**Hybrid approaches** combine both signals. [RLHF](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.02155) (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback) uses human preference labels to learn a reward, then fine-tunes with RL. [IRL](https://ai.stanford.edu/~ang/papers/icml00-irl.pdf) (Inverse RL) recovers a reward function from demonstrations and then runs RL. These are powerful but operationally complex; most robotics practitioners start with pure IL and layer in RL only when they hit a ceiling.

## 2. The Policy Learning Problem

### State Space and Action Space

Let $`\mathcal{S}`$ be the state space—everything needed to describe the world at a given instant. Let $`\mathcal{A}`$ be the action space—the set of commands the robot can issue.

A **policy** maps states to actions:

```math
\pi : \mathcal{S} \rightarrow \mathcal{A} \quad \text{(deterministic)}
```

```math
\pi : \mathcal{S} \rightarrow \Delta(\mathcal{A}) \quad \text{(stochastic; outputs a distribution over actions)}
```

Stochastic policies are critical in IL because expert demonstrations are often *multimodal*: two equally valid ways to pick up an object exist, and a deterministic policy forced to average them will do neither. The diffusion policy and CVAE-based methods (Chapter 3) address this directly.

### Trajectories

A **trajectory** (or rollout) is a sequence of states and actions:

```math
\tau = (s_0, a_0, s_1, a_1, \ldots, s_T, a_T)
```

The initial state $`s_0 \sim \mu_0`$ is drawn from an initial state distribution. Subsequent states follow the (unknown) transition dynamics $`s_{t+1} \sim T(\cdot \mid s_t, a_t)`$.

### The Policy Learning Objective

In behavior cloning, we minimize the average loss on the expert's state distribution:

```math
\min_\theta \; \mathbb{E}_{s \sim d_{\pi^*}} \left[ \ell\!\left(\pi_\theta(s),\, \pi^*(s)\right) \right]
```

where $`d_{\pi^*}`$ is the **state visitation distribution** of the expert policy (the marginal distribution over states when the expert acts). For continuous actions, $`\ell`$ is typically mean-squared error; for discrete tokens it is cross-entropy.

The fundamental challenge: at test time, the policy generates its *own* state visitation distribution $`d_{\pi_\theta}`$, which diverges from $`d_{\pi^*}`$ whenever the policy makes a mistake. This gap grows with time, causing compounding errors (Section 7).

## 3. Why Robot Manipulation Is Hard

Understanding *why* manipulation is a challenging IL domain will inform every design choice in later chapters.

### 3.1 High-Dimensional Continuous Action Spaces

A 7-DoF robot arm (e.g., Franka Panda) has seven joint angles as the primary output. A bimanual system doubles this to 14. Add gripper state and you have 15–16 continuous scalars to predict per timestep. Unlike chess (discrete, finite actions) or Atari (discrete button presses), continuous action spaces require the policy to be precise to within fractions of a degree.

### 3.2 Contact-Rich, Precision-Critical Tasks

Tasks like **insertion** (USB plug, peg-in-hole) or **needle threading** require sub-millimeter accuracy. The policy must model contact forces implicitly—even though most IL setups do not provide force sensing. The ACT system ([Zhao et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.13705)) sidesteps explicit force sensing by using absolute joint position control: the difference between the commanded position and the actual position drives the PID controller, effectively encoding force information in the position error.

### 3.3 Partial Observability

Most real systems observe RGB images, not full state. A wrist-mounted camera cannot see the object it is already grasping; a top-down camera loses depth information. The robot does not know its own internal joint state from vision alone. This is the POMDP setting (Section 4).

### 3.4 Nonstationarity of Human Demonstrations

Different human demonstrators approach the same task differently. Even the same demonstrator varies across sessions. Demonstrations are therefore drawn from a mixture of policies, not a single $`\pi^*`$. This multimodality makes regression-to-the-mean policies fail—averaging a "go left" demonstration and a "go right" demonstration produces a "go straight" policy that collides with obstacles.

### 3.5 Long-Horizon Dependencies

A task like **pick → place → insert** requires coordinated behavior over hundreds of timesteps:

- **Pick:** 200 timesteps to approach, grasp, and lift
- **Place:** 200 timesteps to move to target region
- **Insert:** 200+ timesteps of precision contact

Errors in the pick phase corrupt everything downstream. The effective planning horizon creates temporal credit-assignment challenges even for supervised methods, because the consequence of a small error at $`t=10`$ may not be visible until $`t=350`$.

## 4. Markov Decision Process Framework

### The MDP

An MDP is a tuple $`(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, T, R, \gamma)`$:

- $`\mathcal{S}`$: state space
- $`\mathcal{A}`$: action space
- $`T : \mathcal{S} \times \mathcal{A} \rightarrow \Delta(\mathcal{S})`$: transition dynamics
- $`R : \mathcal{S} \times \mathcal{A} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}`$: reward function
- $`\gamma \in [0, 1]`$: discount factor

**In IL:** $`R`$ is *unknown* (we do not design a reward). $`T`$ is also *unknown* (we do not have a simulator). We work entirely from data $`\mathcal{D}`$.

### The POMDP

A **Partially Observable MDP** (POMDP) extends the MDP with an observation function:

```math
O : \mathcal{S} \rightarrow \Delta(\mathcal{Z})
```

where $`\mathcal{Z}`$ is the observation space (e.g., RGB images). The agent receives observation $`o_t \sim O(\cdot \mid s_t)`$ rather than the true state $`s_t`$.

Because $`o_t \neq s_t`$, a Markov policy $`\pi(o_t)`$ is suboptimal—the agent needs *history* to resolve ambiguity. In practice:

```math
h_t = (o_1, o_2, \ldots, o_t)
```

is used as input. Transformers handle this naturally via attention over the observation sequence. Recurrent networks (LSTM, GRU) are an alternative but have largely been supplanted.

**Practical approximation:** Most IL systems use a short fixed-length history window $`h_t = (o_{t-H}, \ldots, o_t)`$ rather than the full history, which is memory-efficient and sufficient for most manipulation tasks.

## 5. Action Representations

Choosing *how* to represent actions is one of the most consequential design decisions in a manipulation IL system. The right choice depends on your robot, your controller, and your demonstration collection setup.

### 5.1 Joint Position Control (Absolute)

The policy outputs absolute target joint angles $`\mathbf{q}^{\text{target}} \in \mathbb{R}^n`$. The low-level PID controller drives the robot toward these targets.

**Pros:** Simple to demonstrate (move to a target joint state). Captures implicit force: if the commanded position is inside an object, the PID's position error generates contact force automatically.

**Cons:** Absolute positions are frame-dependent. Policies trained on one robot configuration may not transfer to another.

**Used by:** ACT ([Zhao et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.13705)), most ALOHA-based work.

### 5.2 Joint Velocity Control

The policy outputs joint velocities $`\dot{\mathbf{q}} \in \mathbb{R}^n`$. Integration over time produces position.

**Pros:** Smooth trajectories; easier to compose with RL.

**Cons:** Demonstrations require continuous expert velocity input, which is harder to provide naturally. Small errors accumulate via integration.

### 5.3 End-Effector (Cartesian) Control

The policy outputs the desired end-effector pose $`(x, y, z, \text{quat}) \in \mathbb{R}^7`$. Inverse kinematics (IK) solves for joint angles at runtime.

**Pros:** Intuitive for humans to demonstrate (move the hand in space). Transfers across robot morphologies that have similar workspaces.

**Cons:** IK can be non-unique (multiple joint configurations reach the same pose), slow near singularities, and fail at workspace boundaries.

### 5.4 Delta (Relative) Actions

Rather than predicting absolute state, the policy predicts the *change*:

```math
\Delta \mathbf{q}_t = \pi_\theta(h_t), \quad \mathbf{q}_{t+1} = \mathbf{q}_t + \Delta \mathbf{q}_t
```

**Pros:** Locally translation-invariant; easier to generalize across start configurations.

**Cons:** Accumulates integration error over long horizons.

### 5.5 ACT's Design Choice

ACT uses **absolute joint positions** for a specific reason: the ALOHA bimanual system uses a leader-follower teleoperation setup where the demonstrator physically moves a kinematically identical "leader" arm. The follower robot's PID controller tracks the leader's joint positions. The residual between commanded and actual position is not explicitly recorded but is encoded *implicitly* in the trajectory: when the robot pushes against a surface, the joint position error represents the contact force. This makes absolute joint position a surprisingly expressive action representation for contact-rich tasks.

## 6. Observation Representations

### 6.1 Raw RGB Images

The most common observation in modern IL. A single camera produces a $`H \times W \times 3`$ tensor (e.g., $`480 \times 640 \times 3`$). Multi-camera setups—top-down, front-facing, wrist-mounted—provide complementary views that reduce ambiguity.

```python
# Typical image preprocessing in PyTorch
import torchvision.transforms as T

transform = T.Compose([
    T.Resize((224, 224)),         # Resize to backbone input size
    T.ToTensor(),                  # HWC uint8 → CHW float32 in [0,1]
    T.Normalize(mean=[0.485, 0.456, 0.406],
                std=[0.229, 0.224, 0.225]),  # ImageNet stats (for pretrained backbone)
])

# Shape: (N_cams, C, H, W) → flatten to (N_cams * C, H, W) or encode per camera
obs_images = torch.stack([transform(img) for img in camera_frames])  # (N_cams, 3, 224, 224)
```

**Wrist cameras** are the most informative for dexterous tasks: they provide a close-up view of the gripper–object interaction, resolving depth ambiguity that a distant camera cannot.

### 6.2 Point Clouds

A point cloud $`\{(x_i, y_i, z_i, r_i, g_i, b_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}`$ provides explicit 3D geometry. Geometry-aware methods (PointNet++, 3D diffusion policy) operate natively on point clouds.

**Pros:** Invariant to camera pose changes; captures depth explicitly.  
**Cons:** Requires depth sensor; noisier at range; harder to integrate with pretrained 2D vision backbones.

### 6.3 Depth Images (RGBD)

A depth image $`D \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W}`$ paired with an RGB image gives a straightforward 4-channel input. Easier to use than raw point clouds while retaining depth information.

### 6.4 Proprioception

Joint positions $`\mathbf{q}`$, velocities $`\dot{\mathbf{q}}`$, and sometimes torques $`\boldsymbol{\tau}`$ form the proprioceptive observation. This is low-dimensional (7–14 scalars) and cheap to acquire. It is almost always included alongside vision.

```python
# Typical proprioceptive input vector for a 7-DoF arm + gripper
qpos = np.concatenate([
    joint_positions,    # shape (7,) in radians
    joint_velocities,   # shape (7,)
    gripper_state,      # shape (1,): 0=open, 1=closed
])  # total: (15,)
```

### 6.5 Force/Torque Sensors

Six-axis force-torque sensors at the wrist provide contact force and torque vectors. Rarely used in standard IL due to sensor noise and additional calibration burden. **Bi-ACT** ([Chen et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.05981)) is a notable exception, conditioning policies on force signals for contact-rich assembly tasks.

### 6.6 Observation Fusion in Practice

ACT uses: 4 RGB cameras (top, front, two wrists) + joint positions. The image features are extracted by a ResNet-18 backbone, positionally encoded, and concatenated with proprioceptive tokens before passing to the Transformer encoder.

```python
# Schematic observation fusion (ACT-style)
img_features = []
for i, img in enumerate(camera_images):                  # N_cams images
    feat = resnet_backbone(img)                          # (B, D)
    feat = feat + camera_pos_embed[i]                    # add camera identity encoding
    img_features.append(feat)

proprio_token = linear_project(qpos)                     # (B, D)
obs_tokens = torch.stack(img_features + [proprio_token], dim=1)  # (B, N_cams+1, D)
```

## 7. The Compounding Error Problem (Mathematical Treatment)

This is the central challenge of behavior cloning. Understanding it quantitatively will tell you *when* BC is sufficient and *when* you need something more.

### 7.1 The Distribution Shift Argument

Behavior cloning minimizes:

```math
\mathcal{L}_{\text{BC}} = \mathbb{E}_{(s, a) \sim d_{\pi^*}} \left[ \ell(\pi_\theta(s), a) \right]
```

Suppose the trained policy achieves expected per-step loss $`\epsilon`$ on the expert's state distribution $`d_{\pi^*}`$:

```math
\mathbb{E}_{s \sim d_{\pi^*}} [\ell(\pi_\theta(s), \pi^*(s))] \leq \epsilon
```

At deployment, the policy generates its *own* state distribution $`d_{\pi_\theta}`$. Even if $`\pi_\theta`$ is nearly perfect on $`d_{\pi^*}`$, on $`d_{\pi_\theta}`$ it makes mistakes that push the system further from the expert's states—and those novel states make subsequent errors more likely.

### 7.2 The Quadratic Error Bound for BC

**Theorem** ([Ross & Bagnell, 2010](https://arxiv.org/abs/1011.0686)): For a task of horizon $`T`$ and per-step imitation loss $`\epsilon`$ (measured on the expert distribution), the expected total loss under the learned policy satisfies:

```math
\mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T} \ell_t\right] \;\leq\; T\epsilon + O(T^2 \epsilon)
```

**Interpretation:** The linear term $`T\epsilon`$ is what you would get if you could guarantee the policy stays near expert states. The quadratic term $`O(T^2\epsilon)`$ is the compounding cost: errors at early timesteps push the policy into unfamiliar territory, which causes more errors, which push even further. For $`T = 500`$ (a typical manipulation episode) and $`\epsilon = 0.01`$, the quadratic term dominates: $`T^2\epsilon = 2500`$.

### 7.3 The DAgger Bound

**DAgger** (Dataset Aggregation, [Ross et al., 2011](https://arxiv.org/abs/1011.0686)) addresses this by training on states generated by the *learned* policy, not just the expert. After $`N`$ iterations:

```math
\mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T} \ell_t\right] \;\leq\; T\epsilon_{\text{opt}} + O\!\left(T\sqrt{\epsilon_{\text{opt}}}\right)
```

where $`\epsilon_{\text{opt}}`$ is the best achievable loss. The quadratic term is reduced to a square-root term—a massive improvement for large $`T`$.

**Practical cost:** DAgger requires a human expert to be available during training to label the policy's states. This is expensive for physical robots.

### 7.4 Action Chunking Reduces Effective Horizon

**Action chunking** predicts a sequence of $`k`$ actions at once rather than one action per step:

```math
(a_t, a_{t+1}, \ldots, a_{t+k-1}) = \pi_\theta(h_t)
```

The policy queries the model every $`k`$ steps instead of every step, reducing the number of autoregressive decisions from $`T`$ to $`T/k`$. Substituting $`T \leftarrow T/k`$ in the BC bound:

```math
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_t \ell_t\right] \;\leq\; \frac{T}{k}\epsilon + O\!\left(\frac{T^2}{k^2}\epsilon\right)
```

For $`k = 100`$ and $`T = 500`$, the quadratic term shrinks by a factor of $`10{,}000`$. This is the core justification for ACT's action chunking design ([Zhao et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.13705)).

**Temporal ensembling** (Section 8 glossary; Chapter 3) further smooths the transitions between chunks by combining overlapping predictions with exponential weighting.

```python
# Action chunking inference loop (pseudocode)
chunk_size = 100
action_queue = deque()

for step in range(episode_length):
    if len(action_queue) == 0:
        obs = get_observation()
        action_chunk = policy(obs)          # shape: (chunk_size, action_dim)
        action_queue.extend(action_chunk)   # queue up k actions
    
    action = action_queue.popleft()
    env.step(action)
```

## 8. Key Terminology

The table below defines the core vocabulary used throughout this textbook. The "Used in" column points to the method or chapter where the concept is central.

| Term | Definition | Used in |
|---|---|---|
| **Behavior cloning (BC)** | The simplest form of IL: supervised regression from states to expert actions on a static dataset. Suffers from compounding errors. | Ch. 0, 1 |
| **CVAE** | Conditional Variational Autoencoder. A latent-variable generative model used in ACT to capture the multimodal distribution over action sequences. The encoder maps (state, action chunk) → latent $`z`$; the decoder maps (state, $`z`$) → action chunk. | Ch. 3 (ACT) |
| **Action chunking** | Predicting a fixed-length sequence of $`k`$ future actions in a single forward pass, rather than one action per step. Reduces effective horizon from $`T`$ to $`T/k`$, mitigating compounding errors. | Ch. 3 (ACT), Ch. 0 §7 |
| **Temporal ensembling** | At each timestep, combining action predictions from multiple overlapping chunks (each offset by one step) using exponential weighting, so recent predictions contribute more. Smooths the boundary artifacts between chunks. | Ch. 3 (ACT) |
| **VLA** | Vision-Language-Action model. A large pretrained vision-language model (e.g., LLaVA, PaLI) fine-tuned to output robot actions. Examples: RT-2, OpenVLA, π0. Provides semantic generalization via language pretraining. | Ch. 6, 7 |
| **Flow matching** | A generative modeling framework that trains a vector field to transport samples from a simple prior (Gaussian) to a complex target distribution along straight-line paths. More efficient training than DDPM diffusion; used in π0. | Ch. 5 (Diffusion), Ch. 7 (π0) |
| **Diffusion policy** | A policy that models the action distribution as the endpoint of a learned denoising process. At inference, iteratively denoises from Gaussian noise to produce an action (sequence). Handles multimodality well. | Ch. 5 |
| **Tokenization** | Converting continuous actions or observations into discrete tokens for processing by a language model. VLAs tokenize actions as strings of numbers or as special tokens in the vocabulary. Enables use of LLM infrastructure but loses precision. | Ch. 6 (OpenVLA), Ch. 7 |
| **Fine-tuning** | Starting from a pretrained model (e.g., a VLA pretrained on internet video) and continuing training on a task-specific demonstration dataset. Much more data-efficient than training from scratch. | Ch. 6, 7 |
| **LoRA** | Low-Rank Adaptation. A parameter-efficient fine-tuning technique that adds low-rank trainable matrices to frozen pretrained weights. Reduces the number of trainable parameters by 10–100×; used to fine-tune VLAs on limited robot data. | Ch. 6 (OpenVLA) |
| **Co-training** | Training simultaneously on multiple datasets—e.g., robot manipulation data and internet-scale vision-language data—to prevent catastrophic forgetting and improve generalization. Used in π0 and RT-2. | Ch. 7 (π0) |
| **Cross-embodiment** | Training or deploying a single policy across robots with different morphologies (different numbers of joints, different grippers, different cameras). Requires action/observation spaces to be reconciled, e.g., via tokenization or embodiment-specific heads. | Ch. 6, 7, 9 |

## Summary

This chapter established the mathematical and conceptual foundation for the rest of the textbook:

1. **IL learns policies from expert demonstrations** without a reward signal, distinguishing it from both supervised learning (no sequential structure) and RL (no reward needed).
2. **The policy learning problem** is defined over state/action spaces, with the objective of minimizing expected loss under the *policy's own* state distribution—not the expert's.
3. **Manipulation is hard** because of continuous high-dimensional actions, contact physics, partial observability, demonstrator variability, and long horizons.
4. **The MDP/POMDP framework** formalizes the sequential decision problem. In practice, observation history replaces full state.
5. **Action and observation representation choices** are first-class design decisions—not implementation details.
6. **Compounding errors** grow quadratically with horizon under BC, but can be reduced via DAgger (interactive relabeling) or action chunking (reducing effective horizon).
7. **The glossary** (Section 8) defines the vocabulary for all subsequent chapters.
